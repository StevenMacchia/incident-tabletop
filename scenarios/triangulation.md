# The bargains bought with stolen cards

**Sev 2** · Online marketplace · For: Marketplace & e-commerce

> Buyers love the cheap headphones and consoles. Then a big retailer's fraud team calls: the goods were bought with stolen cards, and the orders trace back to your sellers.

## Why this scenario

Fraud using stolen card details to buy online, by phone or by mail order reached 3,196,962 UK cases in 2025, up 13%, with £423.5 million lost. Source: [UK Finance Annual Fraud Report](https://www.ukfinance.org.uk/system/files/2026-06/UK%20Finance%20Fraud%20Report%202026.pdf), 2026.

## Decision 1 · Mon 09:00: A retailer's fraud team calls

A large retailer's investigators say hundreds of its orders, paid with stolen cards, trace back to sellers on your marketplace. Your data points to 85 sellers who joined in the last eight weeks, most with hundreds of sales of consoles and headphones at 20-30% below market. Their tracking numbers come from that retailer, and buyers' photos show its boxes.

A. **Hold payouts to the 85 sellers, confirm the pattern on a sample of orders, and set up a direct line with the retailer's investigators.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ By evening 46 of 50 sampled orders match the retailer's stolen-card list, and about $700,000 in pending payouts stays with you.

B. Ask the retailer to send a formal legal request with evidence before you act against any seller.  
   _What happens:_ It feels careful, but the request takes nine days. Meanwhile payouts go out and every new order triggers another stolen-card purchase.

C. Log the report and leave it to the retailer, since your buyers paid and got their goods.  
   _What happens:_ Your metrics look fine for a week. Then the retailer starts canceling shipments to your buyers, and the sellers cash out and disappear.

**Lesson.** Triangulation fraud hides behind happy buyers: new high-volume sellers, below-market prices and another retailer's tracking are the signal. Hold payouts while you confirm it.

**The law behind it.** The US INFORM Consumers Act requires online marketplaces to collect and verify bank, tax ID and contact details from high-volume third-party sellers: those with 200 or more sales and $5,000 or more in revenue in a 12-month period.

## Decision 2 · Tue 09:00: Thousands of paid orders in limbo

The pattern is confirmed. The 85 sellers have 3,400 open orders from real buyers who have already paid. Every order these sellers fill means another purchase with a stolen card. Your marketplace team warns that canceling them all will flood support and anger loyal buyers.

A. Let the open orders complete so buyers get what they paid for, and block the sellers from taking new ones.  
   _What happens:_ Buyers stay happy, but about 3,400 more stolen-card purchases go through, and the retailer says it is weighing legal action.

B. Ban all 85 sellers and cancel every open order now, using your standard seller-unavailable notice and refund timeline.  
   _What happens:_ The fraud stops, but buyers get a vague email and a 7-day refund wait. Support queues triple and buyers post that you took their money.

C. **Remove the sellers, cancel their open orders, refund buyers in full today from held payouts, and tell buyers plainly what happened.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Some buyers are annoyed to lose a bargain, but most accept the explanation, and the stolen-card purchases tied to your sellers stop.

**Lesson.** Every order a triangulation seller fills creates a new card-fraud victim. Stop the flow, then make buyers whole quickly and honestly; held payouts are what pay for it.

## Decision 3 · Thu 11:00: Everyone wants answers at once

Your payment processor warns that your dispute rate has doubled and is nearing card network limits, driven by buyers whose shipments the retailer canceled. The retailer wants every buyer's name and address, police have opened a case, and a consumer reporter asks what you knew.

A. Send the retailer your full buyer and seller records so it can recover its losses as fast as possible.  
   _What happens:_ The retailer is grateful, but buyers start getting demand letters, and your privacy team flags sharing innocent buyers' data in bulk as a likely privacy breach.

B. **Refund buyers with canceled shipments, send the processor a written plan, share seller evidence with the retailer and police, and tell the reporter the facts.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Disputes fall within two weeks and the processor holds off on penalties. Police link the sellers to a known fraud ring, and the story runs with your response.

C. Fight each dispute case by case and say nothing publicly until police finish their investigation.  
   _What happens:_ Your team loses most disputes, and the processor starts holding back a share of your funds. The reporter runs the story without your side.

**Lesson.** Share what each party needs, not everything you have. Fixing the cause of disputes and giving your processor a plan protects your ability to take payments.

## Decision 4 · Week 3: Fixing how sellers get in

The network is gone. Its sellers needed only an email and a bank account to start, and reached hundreds of sales before anyone checked who they were. Leadership asks what changes before the holiday rush.

A. Block new sellers from listing consoles and headphones until they have six months of history.  
   _What happens:_ This network can't return in those categories, but genuine new sellers go elsewhere and the next ring moves into phones and laptops.

B. Add an automatic rule that flags listings priced more than 20% below market for manual review.  
   _What happens:_ Reviewers drown in genuine clearance deals, and the next ring prices just 15% below market and slips through.

C. **Verify seller identity and bank details before first sale, hold early payouts for fast-growing sellers, flag other retailers' tracking numbers, and share signals with retailers.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Sign-ups dip slightly. A copycat ring is caught in its first week with payouts still held, and the retailer joins a standing signal-sharing channel.

**Lesson.** Triangulation rings need fast access to payouts. Verifying who sellers are, slowing early payouts and sharing signals with retailers remove what makes the scheme pay.

**The law behind it.** In the EU, the Digital Services Act requires online marketplaces to make sure sellers give verified identity information before they can start selling.
