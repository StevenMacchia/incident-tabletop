# The school photo edits

**Sev 1** · AI photo app · For: Generative AI, Social media & video

> A school says your AI editing feature is being used to turn students' photos into sexualized images. Copies are already spreading beyond your app.

## Why this scenario

In 2025, more than 182,000 reports to NCMEC's CyberTipline involved offenders possessing, generating or attempting to generate AI-generated child sexual abuse material. Source: [NCMEC, CyberTipline Data](https://www.missingkids.org/gethelpnow/cybertipline/cybertiplinedata), 2026.

## Decision 1 · Mon 08:30: A school reports edited student photos

A school and three parents report that students' school photos, copied from public profiles, have been turned into sexualized images with your AI editing feature. Your logs confirm the edits were made in your app. The feature drives 35% of daily sessions and anchors this month's ad campaign.

A. Add the wording from the reported requests to your blocklist and keep the feature live while you investigate.  
   _What happens:_ Within hours, logs show users rephrasing requests to get past the list. The school asks why the feature is still on.

B. Take the whole app offline until the investigation is complete.  
   _What happens:_ No new edits are made, but millions of users lose access to their photos, support is swamped and the outage becomes the headline.

C. **Switch off the AI editing feature for everyone now, keep the rest of the app running, and preserve the generation logs.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Daily sessions fall by a third and the ad campaign is paused, but no new edits can be made and the evidence is intact.

**Lesson.** Engagement can recover; harm to a child can't. Switch off the smallest thing that fully stops the harm, and keep the evidence.

## Decision 2 · Mon 16:00: A hotline finds copies elsewhere

A child-protection hotline tells you images carrying your app's watermark are circulating in group chats and on another platform. Your team has traced 212 edited images to nine accounts. An engineer proposes deleting every image and account today so nothing can leak again.

A. **Suspend the nine accounts, report each case to the national child-abuse reporting center, preserve the evidence with restricted access, and share hashes through an industry hash-sharing program.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Reports go in within hours. The other platform blocks matching copies the same day, and investigators later use your preserved logs to identify the account holders.

B. Delete every edited image and the nine accounts today so the material can never leak from your systems.  
   _What happens:_ Your systems are clean, but the evidence investigators need is gone, and you have nothing to hash so other platforms can block copies.

C. Suspend the accounts, but hold off reporting until legal confirms whether AI-edited images are covered by the reporting law.  
   _What happens:_ Legal confirms four days later that they are. Meanwhile copies keep spreading, and your late reports draw questions from investigators.

**Lesson.** Never delete first. Images altered to show a real child count as abuse material: report them, preserve the evidence, and share hashes so other platforms can block copies.

**The law behind it.** Under 18 U.S.C. 2258A, US providers must report apparent child sexual abuse material to NCMEC's CyberTipline as soon as reasonably possible. A completed report is treated as a request to preserve its contents for one year.

## Decision 3 · Tue 11:00: Families, the regulator and the press

Worried parents are forwarding copies they found to the school as proof. The school wants your help writing to families, the regulator has asked for a call today, and a local reporter knows which school is involved.

A. Ask the school to collect copies parents have found and send them to your team so you can find every match.  
   _What happens:_ You find a few more copies, but the images pass through more inboxes, and the school's lawyer warns that forwarding them is itself illegal.

B. **Ask families to stop forwarding copies, give them a named contact and a way to report without sharing images, and brief the regulator before a short public statement.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Families stop circulating copies and use the reporting route. The regulator credits your early briefing, and your statement names no school or child.

C. Issue a holding statement that your safeguards are robust and this was isolated misuse, and route the regulator's questions through your press team.  
   _What happens:_ The reporter learns copies are on another platform and 'isolated' falls apart. The regulator, kept at arm's length, opens a formal inquiry.

**Lesson.** Every forward exposes the child again. Give families a way to act without sharing images, and brief the regulator before the press does.

**The law behind it.** Since May 2026, the US TAKE IT DOWN Act requires covered platforms to remove reported intimate images, including AI-made 'digital forgeries', and known identical copies within 48 hours of a valid request.

## Decision 4 · Week 3: Bringing the feature back

Engagement is down 30%, and leadership wants the editing feature back before the holidays. Your review finds it never checked whether an uploaded photo showed a real person or a child, and was never red-teamed for this misuse.

A. **Relaunch only once the feature blocks sexualized edits of real people, refuses edits to photos that appear to show minors, and passes an external red-team review.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Relaunch slips four weeks and some family-photo edits are refused, but red-team attempts to repeat the misuse fail and the regulator accepts your plan.

B. Relaunch next week with a longer blocked-words list and a new terms-of-use rule against sexualized edits of real people.  
   _What happens:_ Engagement recovers, but within days users find wording the list misses, and the hotline sends a new batch of reports.

C. Relaunch only for paying users who verify they are adults, keeping the current filters.  
   _What happens:_ Misuse dips at first, but checking the uploader's age does nothing for the child in the photo, and reports resume from verified accounts.

**Lesson.** Safeguards must protect the person in the photo, not just check the person using the app. Block sexualized edits of real people, detect minors, and red-team for abuse before launch.

> **Not legal advice.** These notes summarize obligations in plain language to help teams ask the right questions. Laws change and details matter, so confirm with counsel before relying on them.
