# The video-call blackmail ring

**Sev 2** · Dating app · For: Dating

> A ring of fake profiles lures members to video chat, records them and demands money. One report leads to hundreds, and the network is still matching.

## Why this scenario

People aged 20 to 29 filed 22,061 sextortion complaints with the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center in 2025, more than any other age group. Source: [FBI IC3 Annual Report](https://www.ic3.gov/AnnualReport/Reports/2025_IC3Report.pdf), 2026.

## Decision 1 · Mon 08:30: A member reports blackmail

A member reports that a match moved him to a video-chat app within an hour, secretly recorded an intimate call, and now demands money or the recording goes to his contacts. Your team searches and finds 340 similar reports in six weeks, most from members in their twenties. The profiles look different, but their opening messages are nearly identical.

A. Ban the 340 reported profiles today and send each reporter your standard harassment reply.  
   _What happens:_ The profiles are gone by evening, but new ones with the same scripts appear overnight. Reporters get no advice on the blackmail itself.

B. Block video-chat links and phone numbers in all chats across the app while you investigate.  
   _What happens:_ Fast moves off the app slow down, but so do genuine dates. Members complain loudly, and the group switches to spelled-out usernames within two days.

C. **Open an incident, give the reporter clear advice (don't pay, stop replying, keep evidence), preserve all reports, and map the linked profiles today.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ The reporter stops replying and doesn't pay. By evening, matching photos, scripts and devices link 1,100 profiles, far more than were reported.

**Lesson.** One sextortion report is often a window into a network. Support the victim first, preserve evidence, and map the network before you start banning.

## Decision 2 · Tue 09:00: Deciding how wide to sweep

Investigators link 1,100 profiles through AI-generated photos, copied scripts and 40 shared devices. About 900 match on several strong signals; 200 match on one weak signal, such as a photo flagged as possibly AI-edited. New fakes appear daily, and any sweep will tell the group you have found them.

A. Keep watching for another week so you can map every profile and device before acting.  
   _What happens:_ The map gets more complete, but about 70 more members are blackmailed that week, and the group starts moving to new devices.

B. **Remove the 900 in one coordinated sweep, block their devices, preserve evidence, and send the 200 borderline profiles to fast human review.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ The 900 go within an hour and new sign-ups from those devices fail. Review clears 130 real members and removes 70 more fakes.

C. Ban all 1,100 now, plus any account that shares a device or an opening line with them.  
   _What happens:_ The network is gone overnight, but so are about 400 real members. Appeals flood support, and a viral thread calls your bans random.

**Lesson.** Remove networks in one coordinated sweep using several strong signals together. Waiting costs victims, and banning on one weak signal costs innocent members.

## Decision 3 · Wed 11:00: Hundreds of members need help

About 2,300 members chatted with the network, and 400 have reported blackmail. Some paid and were asked for more; several write that they feel desperate. A national police cybercrime unit asks what you can share.

A. **Message all 2,300 with clear steps (don't pay, stop contact, keep evidence), offer takedown help and crisis support, and refer the network to police with preserved evidence.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ More victims come forward and fewer pay. Dozens use your takedown help, and police trace the payment accounts to a crime group abroad.

B. Contact only the 400 members who reported blackmail, so you don't alarm the 1,900 who may not have been targeted.  
   _What happens:_ Reporters get help, but many targeted members never reported out of shame. Some keep paying, and others learn about the network from the news.

C. Tell victims to report to police themselves, and wait for a formal legal request before engaging with the police unit.  
   _What happens:_ Victims bounce between your support team and the police. The legal request takes weeks, and by then the group's payment accounts have closed.

**Lesson.** Paying rarely ends the threats, so tell everyone exposed, not just reporters, what to do, help with takedowns, and give police a case they can act on.

**The law behind it.** Since May 19, 2026, the US TAKE IT DOWN Act requires covered platforms to offer a way to request removal of non-consensual intimate images, and to remove them and known identical copies within 48 hours of a valid request.

## Decision 4 · Week 6: Stopping the next network

The sweep holds, but a smaller copy of the network appears a month later. Your data shows most blackmail cases moved off the app within an hour of matching. Your CEO asks what changes.

A. **Warn members in chat when a new match pushes to move off the app, screen new photos for AI generation, and share network signals with other platforms.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Many members pause after the warning, and blackmail reports fall by more than half. New copycat profiles are caught at sign-up.

B. Require every member to pass a live selfie video check before they can send any message.  
   _What happens:_ Fake profiles drop sharply, but sign-ups fall by a quarter, and within weeks the group buys verified accounts from real people.

C. Publish a sextortion safety guide in the help center and send it to all members in a one-off email.  
   _What happens:_ The guide gets good press, but few members read it, and the copycat network runs the same playbook.

**Lesson.** Sextortion follows a pattern: fast trust, a fast move off the app, then threats. Put warnings at that moment, and share signals so networks can't just move elsewhere.

**The law behind it.** The UK Online Safety Act lists sharing or threatening to share intimate images as a priority offence. Platforms with UK users must take proportionate steps to reduce the risk of their service being used to commit or facilitate it.
