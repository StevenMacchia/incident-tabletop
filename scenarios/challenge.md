# The breath-hold challenge

**Sev 1** · Short-video app, large teen audience · For: Social media & video, Kids & education

> A dangerous "challenge" is spreading fast on a Friday evening. Contain it without wrecking your weekend team or silencing the people warning against it.

## Why this scenario

In Ofcom's late-2025 survey, 30% of UK 11-17-year-olds said they had seen content encouraging them to do dangerous stunts and challenges in the previous four weeks. Source: [Ofcom, Children's Online Experiences](https://www.ofcom.org.uk/siteassets/resources/documents/online-safety/research-statistics-and-data/protecting-children/childrens-online-experiences-research-report.pdf), 2026.

## Decision 1 · Fri 18:40: On-call flags a fast-growing trend

On-call reviewers have seen 40 videos in two hours showing teens holding their breath until they nearly pass out. The hashtag is growing about 300% an hour. A news site abroad has reported a hospitalization linked to it. Your dangerous-acts policy covers this, but nobody has formally classified the trend.

A. **Restrict the hashtag and related searches now, add a safety interstitial, and start a targeted content sweep.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Distribution drops within the hour. A few creators complain that their search results changed.

B. Log it for Monday's policy review. The policy question should be settled before you act.  
   _What happens:_ The hashtag triples over the weekend. By Monday it is trending in three countries.

C. Remove every video using the hashtag, including news reports and parents' warnings.  
   _What happens:_ Reach falls, but journalists and parent groups say they were silenced. Appeals start piling up.

**Lesson.** Contain distribution first (search, hashtags, recommendations), then refine enforcement. Protect counter-speech and news coverage. Over-removal creates a second incident.

**The law behind it.** The UK Online Safety Act lists content encouraging dangerous stunts and challenges as “priority content” harmful to children. Services likely to be accessed by children must protect them from it.

## Decision 2 · Fri 21:30: Volume is outrunning your reviewers

About 12,000 related uploads are waiting. Your overnight shift can handle about 2,000. A near-duplicate matcher could be ready in 45 minutes. The graphic-violence and child-safety queues are also staffed tonight.

A. **Deploy near-duplicate matching to auto-action clear copies. Prioritize everything else for human review by reach.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ The highest-reach videos are reviewed first. QA later finds about 3% false positives, which are restored on appeal.

B. Move reviewers off the other queues, including child safety, so every video gets a human look.  
   _What happens:_ The trend queue clears, but child-safety SLAs are missed overnight. That will come up in your next audit.

C. Remove the videos from recommendations and wait until Monday to review them.  
   _What happens:_ Discovery slows, but the videos still spread through shares and direct links.

**Lesson.** Prioritize by potential reach, not by arrival order. Never pull staff from your highest-severity queues to cover a louder incident.

## Decision 3 · Sat 12:10: Press and parents are asking questions

A national reporter wants comment by 15:00. A large parents' group has tagged your company in a thread that is going viral. Your Comms lead is on a flight.

A. **With Legal and a Comms backup, send a short factual statement: what you're seeing, what you've done, and resources for parents.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ The story quotes your statement and links to your parent resources. The thread calms down.

B. Say "no comment" until the investigation is finished.  
   _What happens:_ The headline reads "Platform silent as dangerous trend spreads".

C. Publish detailed removal numbers and explain exactly how your detection works.  
   _What happens:_ People like the transparency. By Sunday, uploaders are altering their videos to evade the matcher.

**Lesson.** Say quickly what you know and what you're doing. Share outcomes, not detection methods, while the trend is still active.

## Decision 4 · Mon 09:00: The trend is fading

Uploads have dropped 85% from their peak. Your team is exhausted. Executives want to know whether this will happen again.

A. **Hold a blameless post-incident review covering detection time, gaps and false positives. Add the trend to monitoring and brief executives with numbers.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ You leave with three concrete fixes, including a trend watchlist, and a team that feels heard.

B. Lift the restrictions and return to normal work.  
   _What happens:_ Two weeks later a variant appears under a new hashtag, and you start from scratch.

C. Leave every emergency measure in place permanently to be safe.  
   _What happens:_ Unrelated breath-work and freediving creators keep getting suppressed. Complaints continue.

**Lesson.** Emergency measures need an end date and a review. The post-incident review is what makes the next incident smaller.

**The law behind it.** For very large platforms, the EU Digital Services Act (Articles 34 and 35) requires yearly systemic-risk assessments covering harm to minors. Post-incident reviews feed directly into them.

> **Not legal advice.** These notes summarize obligations in plain language to help teams ask the right questions. Laws change and details matter, so confirm with counsel before relying on them.
