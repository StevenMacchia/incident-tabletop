# The vendor goes dark

**Sev 2** · Social network · For: Every company type (tailored)

> A typhoon takes your moderation vendor's main site offline. 60% of your reviewers are gone for a week and reports of harassment, hate and graphic content are piling up.

_This scenario has 8 tailored versions, one per company type. The version below is written for social media & video companies; [play the others live](https://stevenmacchia.com/ts-workbench/#tabletop)._

## Decision 1 · Mon 07:00: Your vendor goes dark

A typhoon has shut your moderation vendor's main site. 60% of your reviewers are offline for at least a week, and reports of harassment, hate and graphic content are piling up.

A. Keep working through reports in the order they arrive.  
   _What happens:_ Some child-safety and threat reports wait two days behind low-risk reports.

B. Switch on automatic removal for everything flagged.  
   _What happens:_ Thousands of wrongful removals, and appeals flood in.

C. **Triage by severity: move your in-house team and remaining vendor staff onto child-safety and threat reports, and pause low-severity work.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Serious cases are still handled within hours while low-risk queues build up.

**Lesson.** In a capacity crisis, severity triage matters more than throughput. Agree your priority tiers before you need them.

## Decision 2 · Mon 12:00: The vendor's people

The vendor's staff are safe, but many have lost power or their homes. The vendor asks whether you'll keep paying during the shutdown.

A. **Keep paying the contract and ask how you can support the staff.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Staff return sooner, and more of them stay afterwards.

B. Stop payment: no work, no pay.  
   _What happens:_ The vendor lays off experienced reviewers you'll need when it reopens.

C. Ask the vendor to move its staff to another city right away.  
   _What happens:_ Staff can't travel, and the request damages the relationship.

**Lesson.** Your vendor's reviewers are part of your safety system. How you treat them in a crisis shapes quality afterwards.

**The law behind it.** Courts in Kenya, the US and Europe have heard cases brought by content moderators about working conditions and exposure to traumatic content.

## Decision 3 · Wed 09:00: The backlog grows

The low-severity backlog has reached 400,000 items, and people are complaining that their reports go unanswered.

A. Close all old reports without action.  
   _What happens:_ People feel ignored, and some harmful content stays up.

B. **Automate clear-cut, low-severity cases with high-accuracy models, spot-check the results, and tell people about the delays.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ The backlog halves in two days, and accuracy holds on the spot checks.

C. Pull engineers and product managers onto the queue.  
   _What happens:_ They make many mistakes, and product work stops.

**Lesson.** Automation is safest on clear-cut, low-harm cases, with spot checks for accuracy and honest messages about delays.

## Decision 4 · Week 3: Never again

Leadership asks how to stop depending on a single site.

A. Bring all moderation in-house immediately.  
   _What happens:_ Costs triple, and hiring takes a year.

B. Negotiate a discount for the outage.  
   _What happens:_ Nothing changes about the risk.

C. **Split review across two vendors in different regions, keep an in-house core for high-severity work, and test the continuity plan twice a year.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ The next regional outage barely affects response times.

**Lesson.** Moderation needs business continuity like any critical system: geographic spread, an in-house core and tested plans.

> **Not legal advice.** These notes summarize obligations in plain language to help teams ask the right questions. Laws change and details matter, so confirm with counsel before relying on them.
