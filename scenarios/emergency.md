# The Saturday night emergency

**Sev 1** · Social network · For: Every company type (tailored)

> Late on a Saturday, a teenager posts that they will end their life tonight, in a post seen only by a few friends. Police then call asking for data without a warrant.

_This scenario has 8 tailored versions, one per company type. The version below is written for social media & video companies; [play the others live](https://stevenmacchia.com/ts-workbench/#tabletop)._

## Decision 1 · Sat 23:10: A report of imminent risk

It's late on a Saturday: a teenager posts that they will end their life tonight, in a post seen only by a few friends. You only know the person's location to city level.

A. **Follow your imminent-risk protocol: show crisis resources, escalate to your on-call lead, and refer to emergency services with the information you hold.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Police carry out a welfare check within the hour. The person is safe and gets support.

B. Log it for the Monday queue.  
   _What happens:_ By Monday, it's too late to help.

C. Suspend the account for breaking the self-harm policy.  
   _What happens:_ The person loses a connection to help at the worst possible moment.

**Lesson.** Imminent-risk reports need a 24/7 protocol. Suspending the person at risk removes help, not harm.

## Decision 2 · Sat 23:40: Police want more

Police call your emergency line asking for the account's phone number, IP address and recent location data, without a warrant.

A. Refuse everything without a court order.  
   _What happens:_ Police lose valuable time trying to find the person.

B. **Use your emergency disclosure procedure: verify the officer, share only what's needed to find the person, and document the decision.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Police find the address quickly, and the disclosure holds up to later review.

C. Hand over everything on the account, including message history.  
   _What happens:_ You disclose far more than needed, which raises privacy and legal problems later.

**Lesson.** Most laws allow emergency disclosure when a life is at risk. Verify the request, share the minimum and document everything.

**The law behind it.** US law (18 U.S.C. § 2702(b)(8)) allows emergency disclosure when there's a good-faith belief of danger of death or serious injury. In the EU, the GDPR permits processing to protect someone's vital interests.

## Decision 3 · Sun 10:00: Looking after your team

The agent who handled the case is shaken. It's their third serious case this month.

A. Thank them and put them back on the queue.  
   _What happens:_ They go on sick leave two weeks later.

B. Move all imminent-risk cases to your legal team.  
   _What happens:_ Lawyers aren't trained for it, and response times slow.

C. **Offer a debrief and counseling, give them time away from high-severity queues, and check exposure across the team.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ They're back on full duties after a week, and two colleagues take up counseling too.

**Lesson.** People who handle crisis cases need structured support. It protects them and keeps response quality high.

## Decision 4 · Week 2: Making the protocol real

Your review finds the protocol worked, but only because a senior person happened to be online.

A. **Set up a staffed 24/7 on-call rotation, train agents to spot imminent risk, and register an emergency contact channel with police in advance.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ The next case is handled in minutes, on a holiday weekend.

B. Write the protocol down in a document.  
   _What happens:_ Nobody can find it the next time it's needed.

C. Leave it to whoever is online at the time.  
   _What happens:_ The next case is missed on a holiday weekend.

**Lesson.** A protocol isn't real until it's staffed, trained and tested outside office hours.

> **Not legal advice.** These notes summarize obligations in plain language to help teams ask the right questions. Laws change and details matter, so confirm with counsel before relying on them.
