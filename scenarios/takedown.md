# The minister's critics

**Sev 2** · Social network · For: Every company type (tailored)

> Journalists' posts criticizing a minister must come down in 24 hours, says a ministry. Your local staff are within reach of the police, and the law it cites could mean anything.

_This scenario has 8 tailored versions, one per company type. The version below is written for social media & video companies; [play the others live](https://stevenmacchia.com/ts-workbench/#tabletop)._

## Decision 1 · Tue 09:00: An official order arrives

Your country manager forwards a letter from the interior ministry. It lists 14 posts by three journalists criticizing a government minister and orders you to remove them within 24 hours. It cites a national security law written broadly enough to cover any criticism, and notes that "responsible persons" at your local office may face consequences. Your country manager asks what to tell her team.

A. Comply immediately and in full to protect your staff.  
   _What happens:_ The demands expand the following week, and users learn you complied quietly.

B. **Have lawyers review the order's legal basis and scope, assess the human-rights impact, and work out what the law strictly requires.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Your lawyers find the order goes further than the law allows. You have options.

C. Ignore it.  
   _What happens:_ Your local office is raided and staff are questioned.

**Lesson.** Handle government orders through a consistent process: check the legal basis and scope, and assess the human-rights impact before acting.

**The law behind it.** The Global Network Initiative Principles and the UN Guiding Principles on Business and Human Rights expect companies to read government demands narrowly and challenge overbroad ones. Under the EU Digital Services Act (Article 9), orders from EU authorities must state their legal basis and territorial scope.

## Decision 2 · Tue 16:00: Protecting people

By 16:00 Legal has read the order. The law covers content that incites violence; two of the 14 posts arguably do, the other twelve are ordinary criticism, and the order says nothing about territory. Your country manager reports two plainclothes officers in the lobby of the local office, and one reviewer has had a visit at home. The team asks whether they are safe.

A. Remove it worldwide to avoid trouble.  
   _What happens:_ People everywhere lose access to legitimate posts, and you're criticized for censorship.

B. Pull your local staff out of the country overnight.  
   _What happens:_ Staff lose their jobs, and some can't leave safely.

C. **Restrict access only in that country and only where the law clearly applies, and formally challenge the rest.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ The posts stay available elsewhere, staff are protected, and you keep your legal position.

**Lesson.** Restrict only what you're legally required to, only where it's required, and keep staff safety central to the plan.

**The law behind it.** Restricting content in one country, rather than removing it globally, is standard practice for local-law demands. Record these restrictions in your transparency reporting.

## Decision 3 · Wed 10:00: Telling the people affected

You have geo-restricted two posts in the country and filed a challenge against the rest. The three journalists have not been told, and one has already posted that her story "vanished" and asked her followers whether you took a bribe. Her editor has emailed your press inbox, and nothing in the order forbids you from telling them.

A. **Tell them what was restricted and why, unless the law forbids it, and explain how they can challenge it.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ They can challenge the decision, and trust holds.

B. Say nothing, to avoid provoking the government.  
   _What happens:_ People assume arbitrary censorship, and trust falls.

C. Post the order publicly on social media.  
   _What happens:_ The government retaliates against your staff.

**Lesson.** Tell affected people where the law allows. It's a core part of accountability for government demands.

**The law behind it.** The EU Digital Services Act (Article 17) requires a statement of reasons whenever content or accounts are restricted, including restrictions made because of a government order.

## Decision 4 · Month 2: Being accountable

Two months on, a coalition of press-freedom and digital-rights groups writes to ask how many government orders you received last year, how you handled them, and whether this one was among them. A reporter has the same questions. Nobody outside your company knows the order existed.

A. Keep government requests confidential.  
   _What happens:_ A leak reveals both the requests and your silence.

B. **Publish government requests in a transparency report, including how many you complied with, narrowed or refused.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Researchers and advocates cite your report as a model.

C. Say you never comply with government requests.  
   _What happens:_ It isn't true, and it's quickly disproved.

**Lesson.** Transparency reporting on government demands builds trust and discourages overreach.

**The law behind it.** The EU Digital Services Act (Articles 15 and 24) requires transparency reports that include the orders received from authorities.

> **Not legal advice.** These notes summarize obligations in plain language to help teams ask the right questions. Laws change and details matter, so confirm with counsel before relying on them.
