# The fourth complaint

**Sev 1** · Ride-hailing app · For: Gig, delivery & rentals

> At 2 a.m., a rider calls your safety line to report that her driver sexually assaulted her. By morning, you learn he had three earlier complaints nobody connected.

## Why this scenario

In 2021 and 2022, two US ride-hail companies reported a combined 52 fatal assaults and 4,469 of the five most serious types of sexual assault, according to the US Government Accountability Office. Source: [US Government Accountability Office](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108552), 2026.

## Decision 1 · Sat 02:10: A rider calls the safety line

A rider calls your in-app safety line to report that her driver sexually assaulted her on a trip that ended 30 minutes ago. She is home, shaken and alone. The driver is online and has just accepted another trip. Trip GPS and in-app messages auto-delete after 30 days.

A. Leave the driver active until an investigator reviews the case on Monday, and have the agent take her full written account tonight.  
   _What happens:_ The driver completes 40 more trips before Monday. She retells every detail to a generalist agent, then stops answering your messages.

B. **Put the driver on a temporary hold, connect her to a trained safety specialist who offers police and support contacts, and preserve the trip's GPS, messages and call logs.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ His pending pickup is reassigned and he can't drive while the case is open. She chooses next steps with a specialist, and the trip record is preserved.

C. Permanently deactivate the driver right away and tell her he has been removed from the platform for good.  
   _What happens:_ She feels believed tonight, but the case is closed with no evidence kept. When the driver appeals, your team has nothing to stand behind.

**Lesson.** A temporary hold manages risk without deciding guilt. In the first hour, support the person reporting and preserve the evidence, because deletion schedules don't pause for investigations.

## Decision 2 · Sat 09:00: Three complaints nobody connected

Your investigator finds three earlier complaints about this driver in eight months: two for inappropriate comments, one for a long late-night route deviation. Each was closed by a different agent with a warning. The driver denies the report, says he relies on the income, and asks to be reinstated today.

A. **Keep the hold, tell the driver the general nature of the report, hear his side, and decide within ten days using trip data and his full history.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Within a week, trip GPS and the complaint pattern support her account. You deactivate him with a written reason and a right to appeal.

B. Reinstate the driver while police decide whether to act, since the earlier complaints were minor and none was ever upheld.  
   _What happens:_ He is back on the road within hours. Ten days later, another rider reports a late-night detour and unwanted comments from him.

C. Keep him on hold with no explanation until police finish their investigation, however long that takes.  
   _What happens:_ Riders are protected, but months pass with no decision. The driver files repeated appeals, a drivers' group takes up his case, and your own review stalls.

**Lesson.** Police timelines aren't yours. Run a fair process of your own on the evidence you hold, and review past complaints as a pattern, not one at a time.

## Decision 3 · Mon 11:00: The detective and the rider

A detective asks for the driver's identity, the trip's GPS and messages, and "everything you have on both of them." Meanwhile, your automated systems have asked the rider to rate the trip. She writes back asking whether anyone is actually handling her case.

A. Send the detective everything on both accounts right away, including her full trip history, to show you are cooperating fully.  
   _What happens:_ The case moves, but two years of her unrelated trips end up in a file that the driver's defense lawyer can later see.

B. Insist on a warrant for any data, and route all contact with the rider through legal so nothing you say affects the case.  
   _What happens:_ The warrant takes three weeks. Her only messages from you are formal legal letters, and she tells the detective she feels abandoned.

C. **Share the trip's GPS, messages and driver details once valid legal process arrives, switch off automated prompts to her, and assign her one named specialist.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ The detective has what the case needs within days. She hears from one person, is never asked to retell her account, and keeps working with police.

**Lesson.** Share what the case needs under proper legal process, not everything you hold. One trained contact, and never making her retell it, is what trauma-informed support looks like.

## Decision 4 · Week 3: Why the pattern was missed

The driver is deactivated and the police case continues. Your review finds complaints are filed by trip, so no one ever saw a driver's history in one place. Criminal record checks rerun only once a year. Your CEO wants a plan by Friday.

A. Automatically deactivate any driver after a single complaint about inappropriate comments or route deviation.  
   _What happens:_ Hundreds of drivers lose their income over traffic detours and false reports. Appeals swamp your team, and drivers start refusing late-night trips.

B. **Link every complaint to the driver's full history with escalation rules, add continuous criminal-record monitoring, and build in-trip safety tools for riders and drivers alike.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Patterns reach an investigator by the second complaint. Drivers, who face assaults from riders too, get the same emergency button and audio recording.

C. Launch a rider emergency button and a public safety report this month, and plan the complaint-system changes for next year.  
   _What happens:_ The launch gets good coverage. But complaints are still reviewed one trip at a time, and two months later another pattern slips through.

**Lesson.** Low-level complaints are often early warnings, so review them as one history, not trip by trip. Build the same protections for drivers, who are assaulted by riders too.

**The law behind it.** In Texas, the ride-hail law (Occupations Code Chapter 2402) requires companies to rerun criminal background and driving history checks on every driver at least once a year. Continuous monitoring goes beyond that minimum.

> **Not legal advice.** These notes summarize obligations in plain language to help teams ask the right questions. Laws change and details matter, so confirm with counsel before relying on them.
