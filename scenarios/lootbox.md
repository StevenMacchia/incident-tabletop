# The $3,000 bill

**Sev 2** · Mobile game · For: Gaming

> A 12-year-old spends $3,000 on loot boxes in two weeks, the parent's post goes viral, and a regulator starts asking questions.

## Why this scenario

In January 2025 a popular game's maker agreed to pay $20 million and block under-16s from in-game purchases without parental consent, settling FTC allegations it deceived children about in-game costs and rare-prize odds. Source: [US Federal Trade Commission](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/01/genshin-impact-game-developer-will-be-banned-selling-lootboxes-teens-under-16-without-parental), 2025.

## Decision 1 · Mon 09:00: A parent's complaint

A parent's card statement shows their 12-year-old spent $3,000 on randomized item packs in two weeks, using a saved card. The parent posts it online and it goes viral.

A. Refuse: the purchases were made with a saved card.  
   _What happens:_ The story spreads, and a regulator takes notice.

B. **Refund the purchases, contact the parent, and add a spending limit to the child's account.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ The parent posts an update thanking you.

C. Refund quietly and ask the parent to delete the post.  
   _What happens:_ The parent posts your request too.

**Lesson.** When children overspend, generous refunds and immediate controls cost far less than the damage to your reputation.

## Decision 2 · Tue 10:00: Why was it possible?

Your data shows 4% of under-16 players spent more than $500 last year, and purchases don't require a password.

A. Leave it to parents to set up controls.  
   _What happens:_ Few parents know the controls exist.

B. Stop selling to under-18s entirely.  
   _What happens:_ Children lie about their age, and revenue falls without solving the problem.

C. **Require a password or parent approval for every purchase by under-18s, with monthly spending limits on by default.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Big spends by children drop sharply, with little effect on adult revenue.

**Lesson.** Protections that are on by default work better than controls parents don't know about.

**The law behind it.** The FTC's 2022 settlement with Epic Games included $245 million in refunds for design that let children make purchases without parental consent.

## Decision 3 · Week 2: The regulator's letter

A consumer regulator asks whether your randomized packs are designed to pressure children into buying, and whether the odds are disclosed.

A. **Answer openly, publish drop odds, remove countdown timers and “last chance” prompts for children, and show purchase history clearly.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ The regulator closes the inquiry with recommendations.

B. Argue that loot boxes aren't gambling.  
   _What happens:_ The regulator notes you didn't answer its questions.

C. Remove randomized packs in that one country.  
   _What happens:_ Regulators elsewhere take note.

**Lesson.** Regulators focus on design that pressures children. Disclose odds and remove manipulative prompts.

**The law behind it.** Belgium treats paid loot boxes as gambling, Australia rates games with paid loot boxes at least M (from September 2024), and Brazil's ECA Digital bans loot boxes for minors.

## Decision 4 · Month 3: The next season

Product is planning a new season with a limited-time pack.

A. Launch it as planned.  
   _What happens:_ Complaints from parents spike again.

B. **Review the design for manipulative patterns before launch, and build in child protections.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ The season launches with fewer complaints and steady revenue.

C. Cancel the season.  
   _What happens:_ Players are disappointed and revenue falls.

**Lesson.** Build a design and safety review into every launch aimed at young players.

> **Not legal advice.** These notes summarize obligations in plain language to help teams ask the right questions. Laws change and details matter, so confirm with counsel before relying on them.
