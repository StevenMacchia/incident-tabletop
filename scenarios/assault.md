# The date that went wrong

**Sev 1** · Dating app · For: Dating

> A user reports being sexually assaulted by a match. The accused is still active and has more dates lined up.

## Decision 1 · Sun 10:00: A report of assault

A user reports that a match sexually assaulted her on a first date last night. She has told the police. The accused's account is active, and he has three dates arranged this week.

A. Ask her for proof before you act.  
   _What happens:_ She stops responding. The accused goes on his dates.

B. Warn his upcoming matches that someone has reported him.  
   _What happens:_ It may breach privacy and defamation law, and the warning leaks.

C. **Suspend the accused while you investigate, support the reporter with resources, and preserve evidence for police.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ No further dates happen, and police ask for the preserved data.

**Lesson.** For serious offline-harm reports, suspend during the investigation, support the person who reported, and preserve evidence.

**The law behind it.** In the US, law enforcement can ask providers to preserve records for 90 days, renewable (18 U.S.C. § 2703(f)). For serious offline harm, preserve evidence proactively instead of waiting to be asked.

## Decision 2 · Mon 14:00: The accused appeals

The accused says it was consensual and threatens to sue. Police are investigating but won't share details.

A. **Keep the suspension under your safety policy, which allows action on credible reports without a criminal conviction.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ His lawyer backs off after reading your policy.

B. Reinstate him until the police finish.  
   _What happens:_ He matches with more users, and a second report arrives.

C. Delete his account and data immediately.  
   _What happens:_ The police investigation loses important evidence.

**Lesson.** Your safety standard isn't a criminal court's. A clear policy lets you act on credible risk while preserving evidence.

**The law behind it.** Platforms can enforce their own safety policies without a criminal conviction, as long as the terms are clear and applied consistently.

## Decision 3 · Tue 09:00: He's back

A new account with different photos, but the same phone number and device, starts matching in the same area.

A. Require ID checks for everyone in that city.  
   _What happens:_ He's blocked, but so are many real users, who drop off.

B. **Ban the new account and link it to the original case.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ You block him again within hours, and the case record stays complete.

C. Wait for a new report.  
   _What happens:_ He goes on a date before anyone reports him.

**Lesson.** Ban evasion is predictable after serious bans. Device, phone and payment signals make repeat offenders catchable.

## Decision 4 · Week 3: A reporter calls

A journalist investigating assaults linked to dating apps asks what you do to prevent them.

A. **Explain your safety features (photo verification, date check-ins, how reports are handled) and publish safety numbers.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ The article names you as one of the more responsive apps.

B. Point them to your community guidelines.  
   _What happens:_ The story says you “declined to explain”.

C. Offer an exclusive on a new feature launch instead.  
   _What happens:_ The reporter sees it as deflection.

**Lesson.** Offline harm is the defining risk for dating apps. Be ready to explain prevention and response in specifics.

> **Not legal advice.** These notes summarize obligations in plain language to help teams ask the right questions. Laws change and details matter, so confirm with counsel before relying on them.
