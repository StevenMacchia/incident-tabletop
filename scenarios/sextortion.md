# The gift card ultimatum

**Sev 1** · Teen social app · For: Social media & video, Gaming, Kids & education

> Fake accounts posing as teenage girls target boys at one high school, get an image, then demand gift cards. Within hours, one boy posts that he wants to die.

## Why this scenario

NCMEC received an average of 137 financial sextortion reports a day in 2025, up 37% on 2024, and knows of at least three dozen US teenage boys who took their lives after being targeted. Source: [NCMEC, CyberTipline Data](https://www.missingkids.org/gethelpnow/cybertipline/cybertiplinedata), 2026.

## Decision 1 · Mon 21:00: Scripted accounts swarm one school

In two hours, 40 new accounts posing as teenage girls follow hundreds of students at one high school. Three boys report the same script: a friendly opener in a game chat or comment thread, a move to direct messages, then a demand for gift cards and a threat to send an image to everyone they know.

A. Ban the three reported accounts now and send each boy your standard guide to blocking and reporting.  
   _What happens:_ The three accounts are gone in minutes. The other 37 keep messaging students, and new reports arrive all night.

B. **Link the accounts by device, sign-up pattern, scripts and payment handles, remove the whole network, preserve evidence, file mandatory reports and alert every student they contacted.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ One sweep removes 52 linked accounts, including 12 no one had reported. Targeted students get a safety notice and reports are filed by morning.

C. Pause direct messages from all accounts under seven days old, platform-wide, until the morning review.  
   _What happens:_ The campaign stalls overnight, but so do thousands of real new users. By morning, the offenders switch to older accounts.

**Lesson.** Sextortion is usually run as an organized campaign. Remove the whole network on shared signals, warn the people it targeted, and preserve and report as you go.

**The law behind it.** In the US, 18 U.S.C. 2258A requires providers to report apparent child sexual abuse material and enticement of minors to NCMEC's CyberTipline as soon as reasonably possible. The 2024 REPORT Act extended preservation of reported content from 90 days to one year.

## Decision 2 · Tue 01:15: A target says he wants to die

Earlier tonight, one 15-year-old target paid $50 in gift cards, and the demand doubled. Now he posts a story saying he wants to die and that everyone will see the image. Your classifier flags the post while he is online.

A. Remove the story under your self-harm policy and send him the standard automated support message.  
   _What happens:_ The story is gone in seconds, but the message reads like a rule warning. He goes quiet, and you don't know if he is safe.

B. Lock his account to protect him and email the parent address on file to explain what happened.  
   _What happens:_ The email reaches an address he set up himself. Locked out, he loses the friends who were reaching out and still fears the image will spread.

C. **Have a trained specialist message him now: connect him to a crisis line, tell him it's not his fault and not to pay, and request an emergency welfare check.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ He replies to the specialist and agrees to call the crisis line. Police confirm he is safe at home with a parent within the hour.

**Lesson.** When a target says he wants to die, a fast, human response matters most. Tell him it isn't his fault and not to pay, and connect him to crisis support now.

## Decision 3 · Tue 10:00: His family, police and school call

Last night, the offender sent the image to three of the boy's followers. His parents want it gone for good, here and elsewhere. A detective asks what you have. The principal wants to warn every family and asks for the names of all targeted students.

A. **Hash and remove every copy, help the family use a cross-platform hash service, route police to your preserved evidence, and help the school warn families without names.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Copies come down within hours and are blocked on participating platforms. The detective has what she needs, and the school's warning names no one.

B. Remove the copies your team finds and tell the family to report anything on other sites to police.  
   _What happens:_ Your platform is clean by evening, but a copy surfaces on another site and the family feels left on their own.

C. Send the principal the list of targeted students so the school can call each family today.  
   _What happens:_ Families hear fast, but the list leaks to a student group chat and the targeted boys are named and teased.

**Lesson.** Victims need the image gone everywhere, not just on your platform. Use hashing and shared hash lists, work with police through your preserved evidence, and keep every target's identity private.

**The law behind it.** Under the US TAKE IT DOWN Act, enforced by the FTC since May 19, 2026, covered platforms must remove non-consensual intimate images and known identical copies within 48 hours of a valid removal request.

## Decision 4 · Week 3: Closing the door on strangers

The campaign is over. Your review finds offenders used public follower lists to find targets and make their threats credible, and messaged teens freely from accounts made that day. Leadership wants changes before next term but worries about growth.

A. Add a sextortion warning banner to teens' inboxes and leave messaging and privacy settings as they are.  
   _What happens:_ Teens stop noticing the banner within a week, and the next campaign uses the same public follower lists.

B. **By default for teens, limit messages from accounts they don't follow, hide friend and follower lists from strangers, and blur suspected nudity behind a warning.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Unwanted messages to teens drop sharply, and the next campaign struggles to find targets. Some teens complain about the limits, and growth dips slightly.

C. Require a government ID check for every new account before it can send any message.  
   _What happens:_ Fake accounts slow down, but sign-ups fall, privacy groups object to collecting teens' IDs, and offenders buy older accounts instead.

**Lesson.** Sextortion depends on easy access to teens and their friend lists. Default protections that limit what strangers can see and send work better than warnings alone or blanket ID checks.
