# The recalled toy

**Sev 1** · Online marketplace · For: Marketplace & e-commerce

> A toddler is hospitalized after swallowing magnets from a toy bought on your marketplace. The toy was recalled months ago.

## Why this scenario

In 2025 the EU's eSurveillance web crawler scanned over 1.6 million websites and found more than 20,800 products for sale online that were already listed as dangerous in the EU Safety Gate alert system. Source: [European Commission, Safety Gate report](https://ec.europa.eu/commission/presscorner/detail/en/ip_26_537), 2026.

## Decision 1 · Thu 08:00: A child is hurt

A parent reports their toddler was hospitalized after swallowing small, powerful magnets from a toy bought on your marketplace. Regulators recalled the toy four months ago, and 300 listings from 40 sellers are still live.

A. Remove the one listing the parent bought from.  
   _What happens:_ The other 299 listings keep selling.

B. **Remove every listing matching the recalled product, stop pending orders, and contact the parent to offer support.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Every matching listing is down by noon, and 60 pending orders are stopped before shipping.

C. Ask sellers to confirm their stock isn't the recalled version.  
   _What happens:_ Most don't reply, and sales continue for a week.

**Lesson.** When a product can hurt children, remove first and verify later. One report usually points to many listings.

## Decision 2 · Thu 14:00: Buyers who already have it

2,100 people bought the toy in the past four months. Many are parents of young children.

A. **Email past buyers directly with the recall notice, how to get a refund, and what to do if a child may have swallowed a magnet.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Parents return the toy, and a poison-control center thanks you for the clear guidance.

B. Post the recall notice in your help center.  
   _What happens:_ Almost none of the buyers see it.

C. Leave it to the manufacturer to contact buyers.  
   _What happens:_ The manufacturer doesn't have buyers' contact details. You do.

**Lesson.** Marketplaces often hold the only contact details for buyers. Use them to reach people when there's a safety recall.

## Decision 3 · Fri 10:00: Why didn't you catch it?

The consumer-safety regulator asks why recalled products were still listed four months after the recall.

A. Say sellers are responsible for what they list.  
   _What happens:_ The regulator reminds you of your own obligations and opens an inquiry.

B. **Explain honestly, and commit to checking listings against official recall databases daily, with automatic removal of matches.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ The regulator accepts the plan and schedules a follow-up.

C. Promise to review every listing by hand.  
   _What happens:_ It's impossible at your scale, and the regulator knows it.

**Lesson.** Checking listings against official recall databases is a basic control for any marketplace selling physical goods.

**The law behind it.** The EU General Product Safety Regulation (applied since December 2024) requires online marketplaces to act on authorities' orders about dangerous products within two working days. In the US, the Consumer Product Safety Commission coordinates recalls and can pursue sellers of recalled products.

## Decision 4 · Month 2: High-risk categories

Children's products, electronics and cosmetics account for most of your safety incidents.

A. Treat all categories the same.  
   _What happens:_ The next incident is another children's product.

B. Stop selling children's products entirely.  
   _What happens:_ Legitimate sellers leave, and buyers go elsewhere.

C. **Require safety certificates from sellers in high-risk categories, and screen new listings in those categories before they go live.** ✅ strongest call  
   _What happens:_ Unsafe listings in those categories drop sharply.

**Lesson.** Put your strongest controls on the categories where products can physically hurt people.

> **Not legal advice.** These notes summarize obligations in plain language to help teams ask the right questions. Laws change and details matter, so confirm with counsel before relying on them.
